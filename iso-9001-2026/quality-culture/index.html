Quality culture and ethical behavior in ISO 9001:2026: what the requirement is, and what counts as evidence

Two new words in the standard, and the first thing a surveillance auditor will ask a machinist about. Quality culture and ethical behavior went from a background idea in 2015 to a leadership duty and an awareness requirement in 2026. Nothing about them can be satisfied on paper, which is why they are the change most likely to produce a finding.

Where the requirement lives

Culture appeared once in the 2015 edition, as one of the internal issues you might consider when working out your context. In 2026 it appears as a requirement in two places and as context in three more.

ClauseWhat it says, in plain wordsType
5.1.1 i)Top management has to promote quality culture and ethical behavior. It sits in the list of things leadership must demonstrate, alongside promoting the process approach and risk-based and opportunity-based thinking. A new note says an organization's culture and ethics are visible in what people value, how they think about their work, what they do routinely, and the actions they take.Requirement
7.3 e)Everyone who works for you, or on your behalf, has to be aware of the quality culture and ethical behavior the organization expects. It joins the policy, their contribution, the cost of nonconformance, and now the relevant objectives.Requirement
7.1.4 noteThe work environment note keeps its social, psychological and physical factors and adds that culture and ethical behavior can shape some of them.Context
4.1 noteValues and culture remain examples of internal issues to consider, now alongside strategic direction and resources.Context
Annex A (informative)Says leadership is central to promoting culture and awareness, that ethical behavior in decisions and interactions can affect every part of quality, that the policy can reflect the culture the organization expects, and points to ISO 10010 for guidance on quality culture and ISO 10018 for people engagement.Guidance

The verbs matter. Leadership must promote. People must be aware. Neither clause says document, measure, survey or certify. An auditor who asks for your culture procedure is asking for something the standard does not require. An auditor who asks a welder what the company expects of him is doing exactly what the standard requires.

What it is not

Most of the transition work I have seen on this clause so far is wasted. Three things it does not ask for:

  • A culture program. No survey, no maturity model, no posters. If you run one, fine, but a survey score is not the evidence and an auditor cannot require one.
  • A new document. There is no documented-information requirement attached to either clause. The policy can carry a line about expected behavior (Annex A suggests it can), and 5.2.2 now asks that the policy be put into practice, understood and followed, which is the closest the standard comes to asking you to write culture down.
  • A definition. The standard does not define quality culture. The note under 5.1.1 tells you where to look for it instead: what people value, how they think about the work, what they habitually do, and the actions they take. That is a list of things that can be observed and asked about, not filed.

What it does ask for is harder: that the way your plant actually behaves when something goes wrong is something top management has shaped on purpose, and that the people on the floor can tell an auditor what that expected behavior is.

The three answers a machinist gives

Ask a machinist what the company expects from him when a part is out of tolerance. Every answer I have ever heard falls into one of three kinds.

1. "I don't know" or "nobody's told me"

A finding against 7.3 e), and probably against 7.3 c) as well. It usually means the awareness training exists as a signed sheet and not as a conversation. The fix is not another sheet.

2. The slogan

"Quality is everyone's responsibility." "Right first time." He has read the wall. An auditor will accept it as awareness of the policy and then ask the follow-up: what did you do the last time a part was out? If the answer is a shrug, the slogan has no culture behind it.

3. What he actually does

"I stop the job, tag the parts, and tell the lead. Last month I flagged a bore that was drifting and they pulled the tool before it made scrap." That is the evidence. It names a practice, an action, and what happened next. An auditor corroborates it in the records: the nonconformity report raised from the floor, the hold tag, the corrective action that went to the tool instead of the operator. The note under 5.1.1 lists exactly these things: practices and actions.

The third answer cannot be trained in a session. It exists when stopping a job has never cost anyone anything, when the last person who raised a problem was thanked in front of the shift, and when the corrective action register shows causes rather than names. Those are leadership behaviors, which is why the requirement sits in 5.1.1 before it sits in 7.3.

What top management has to show under 5.1.1

Promotion is an activity, so the evidence is what leaders did, not what they signed. An auditor looks for it in five places.

  1. The last conflict between quality and delivery. Every plant has one each quarter. What was decided, who decided it, and does the floor know? If schedule pressure overrode a hold and nobody wrote down why, the culture the auditor sees is the one that shipped the part.
  2. How corrective actions close. The 10.2 register is a culture record. Causes that stop at a person's name, retraining as the standard fix, and effectiveness checks that say "done" tell an auditor what leadership rewards.
  3. Management review. Nothing in 9.3 requires a culture agenda item. But minutes that discuss escapes, near misses raised from the floor, and what leadership did about the environment are the easiest 5.1.1 i) evidence there is. Minutes that only show the scorecard are not.
  4. Resource decisions. A gauge that stayed broken for a quarter, an inspection step cut to hit rate, an audit program half completed: each is a leadership action that shaped culture, and each is visible in records the auditor already has.
  5. Presence. Whether the plant manager has been on the floor when a problem was raised, and what she said. Auditors ask operators this question directly.

One question for the plant manager: tell me about the last time someone stopped a job. What happened to them? If the honest answer is "nothing, and we found the cause," 5.1.1 i) is covered. If it takes a moment to think of a time anyone stopped a job, that is the finding.

The ethics half

Ethical behavior is paired with culture in every place it appears, and it is the half that most quality manuals have never addressed. Annex A frames it as behavior in decisions, actions and interactions that can affect every aspect of quality and the confidence interested parties have in you. On the floor it is specific:

  • Inspection records completed without the inspection. The pencil-whipped checklist is an ethics failure before it is a 7.1.5 failure.
  • Known nonconforming product shipped on the assumption the customer will not notice, or with a certificate that says something the test did not show.
  • Audit findings softened because the auditee is a friend, or internal audits scheduled where the auditor already knows the answer.
  • Supplier data accepted because checking it would delay the order.

Every large quality failure on the Lead Time channel has one of these at its center, and in each case the paperwork was in order. That is why the clause exists. An auditor who has read the standard's rationale will ask what your organization expects when the honest record is the inconvenient one, and whether anyone has ever been rewarded for producing it.

How an auditor will test it

Expect the interview walk, not a document request. A typical pattern:

  1. Three people in three roles: an operator, a lead or supervisor, and someone in a support function such as purchasing or shipping. Each is asked what the organization expects of them on quality and on honesty, and what happens when something is wrong.
  2. The answers are checked against each other. Three different descriptions of the expected behavior is a 5.1.1 i) question: leadership has not promoted one culture.
  3. The answers are checked against records: nonconformity reports raised by operators in the last quarter, hold tags, the corrective action register, the last management review, the audit program.
  4. Top management is asked the same questions in reverse: what do you expect, how do you promote it, and show me the last time it cost you something to hold to it.
  5. The work environment is walked with 7.1.4 in mind. A shop where reporting a defect draws a raised voice is a psychological factor the note now ties to culture.

The finding, when it comes, is rarely "no quality culture." It is 7.3 e) for people who cannot describe what is expected, or 5.1.1 i) for leadership that cannot show it did anything to promote it.

Closing the gap in a month

  1. Write down what you expect, in one page. Not a values statement. Five or six behaviors in plain words: stop the job when in doubt, record what you saw and not what you wished you saw, raise problems to the lead the same shift, never sign for a check you did not do, treat the supplier's data as a claim until verified. Put it in or beside the quality policy so 5.2.2 d) and 7.3 e) are covered by the same document.
  2. Brief every person, in person. A ten-minute toolbox talk by the area lead, with the page in hand and one real example from the plant. Keep the attendance record; that is your 7.3 evidence. Do not e-mail it.
  3. Fix the one behavior that contradicts it. Every plant has one: the hold that gets overridden, the check that gets signed in advance, the operator who was blamed last time. Fix that one visibly. It is worth more than the other four steps together.
  4. Add three interview questions to the internal audit checklist. What does the company expect of you on quality? What do you do when a part is wrong? What happened last time? Your 9.2 audits then generate the evidence before the certification body asks for it. The free internal audit checklist on this site has a place for them.
  5. One visible leadership act. The plant manager closes a corrective action personally, or thanks the person who stopped the job in front of the shift, or reverses a schedule decision in favor of a hold and says why. Then it goes in the management review minutes.

Questions

Does ISO 9001:2026 require a quality culture program?

No. It requires top management to promote quality culture and ethical behavior (5.1.1), and it requires everyone who works for you or on your behalf to be aware of the quality culture and ethical behavior the organization expects (7.3). Neither clause asks for a program, a survey or a document. Both are audited by interview and by what the records show people actually did.

Do I need a code of conduct for ISO 9001:2026?

Not as a requirement. If you already have one, it is useful evidence for the ethical-behavior half, provided people know it exists and it matches how the plant is actually run. A code nobody can describe, or one contradicted by what happened the last time a defect was found, is worse than none.

How will an auditor audit quality culture?

By asking people what the organization expects of them and what happens when something is wrong, then checking the answer against records: nonconformity reports raised from the floor, jobs stopped, corrective actions that went to a cause rather than a name, and how top management handled the last conflict between quality and delivery. The standard's own note says culture and ethics show in what people value, how they think, and what they do, which is where the auditor will look.

Is there a record required for quality culture or ethical behavior?

No specific record. Awareness under 7.3 is usually evidenced by briefing or training records plus interviews, and leadership under 5.1.1 by what management review minutes, communications and corrective action decisions show. The standard does not add a documented-information requirement for either.

What is ISO 10010?

A guidance document on understanding, evaluating and improving organizational quality culture. ISO 9001:2026 points to it in the informative Annex A as a source of help. It is not a requirement and an auditor cannot cite it against you.
