ISO 9001 — CLAUSE 7.5

ISO 9001 clause 7.5, documented information: what has to exist, how it has to be controlled, and the one wording change in 2026 that touches every procedure

Documented information is the clause that replaced documents and records in 2015, and the one the 2026 edition changed the wording of everywhere else in the standard. It is also the source of the cheapest findings in any audit: the uncontrolled form on the shop floor, the procedure with no revision, the record that cannot be found. This page covers what the clause requires, how it is audited, and the sweep to run before the next audit.

What clause 7.5 requires

Sub-clauseIn plain words
7.5.1 GeneralYour system must include the documented information the standard requires, and whatever else you decide is necessary for the system to work. A note says how much that is depends on your size, the complexity of your processes and the competence of your people. There is no required manual and no required set of procedures.
7.5.2 Creating and updatingWhen you create or update documented information, make sure it is identified and described (title, date, author, reference), in a suitable format and medium, and reviewed and approved as suitable and adequate.
7.5.3 ControlDocumented information must be available at the place and time it is needed, and protected from loss of confidentiality, misuse or loss of integrity. As applicable, address distribution, access, retrieval and use; storage and preservation, including keeping it legible; change control, such as version control; and retention and disposition. Documented information from outside that you need for planning and operation, such as customer drawings and standards, must be identified and controlled. Evidence of conformity must be protected from unintended alteration. A note says access can mean permission to view, or permission to view and change.

Two kinds of documented information, one clause. Information you maintain (procedures, work instructions, drawings) has to be current, approved and at the point of use. Information you keep as evidence (records) has to be retrievable, legible and protected from change. The 2015 edition called these "maintain" and "retain." The 2026 edition changed that wording, which is the part that touches your procedures.

What changed in 2026

Clause 7.5 itself barely moved: 7.5.2's title now names documented information, and 7.5.3 says evidence of conformity is to be "available" rather than "retained." What changed is everywhere else. Throughout the standard, the phrases "retain documented information" and "maintain documented information" were replaced with wording about documented information being available, or available as evidence. Annex A explains which is which. The records you keep are the same; the words you used to cite the requirement are not. Any procedure that quotes the 2015 phrasing, and any document-control procedure that defines its scope by "maintain" and "retain," has a sentence to update. What an auditor checks

  1. Point of use. The auditor picks a work instruction on the floor and compares it to the master. Revision, approval, and whether the copy at the machine is the current one.
  2. The list of what you decided is necessary. 7.5.1 b) is a decision. An auditor asks how you decided which processes need documented information and at what level of detail, and looks for processes that plainly need it and have none.
  3. Retrieval. "Show me the inspection record for this serial number" and a stopwatch. Records that exist but cannot be found in reasonable time fail the retrievability test.
  4. External documents. The customer drawing, the standard, the regulation: which revision you hold, how you learn it changed, and whether the floor has the old one.
  5. Protection of evidence. Records that can be edited after the fact with no trace, shared spreadsheets with no access control, paper records in a damp cabinet.
  6. Retention and disposition. How long each record is kept, who decided, and what happens to it after.

The findings that come up most

  • Uncontrolled documents at the point of use. A printout from three revisions ago taped to the machine.
  • Forms without identification. No title, number, revision or approval.
  • External documents at the wrong revision, most often customer drawings.
  • Editable records. Inspection results in a spreadsheet anyone can change.
  • Procedures still citing the 2015 clause numbers or phrasing after the 2026 edition: "10.3," "retain documented information," "6.1.2" for opportunities.
  • A quality manual nobody uses, kept because someone believes the standard requires it. It does not, and it is where stale clause references live.

The last two findings are the ones the 2026 edition created, and they are mechanical to clear. How to control documented information without drowning in it

  1. Keep less. 7.5.1 b) lets you decide what is necessary. A competent crew with a good work instruction needs fewer documents than a new crew with a bad one. Retire what nobody reads.
  2. Identify everything the same way. Title, number, revision, date, approver, on every document and form, in the same place.
  3. Control the point of use, not the file server. The master list matters less than whether the copy at the machine is current. Walk the floor with the list once a quarter.
  4. Register external documents with a revision and an owner whose job is to know when they change.
  5. Protect records from edits. Lock the sheet, or move to a system that logs changes. An inspection record that can be changed silently is not evidence.
  6. Run the clause-reference sweep after every edition change. Search for the old numbers and phrases, fix the references, re-approve, and keep the search results as evidence that the system was reviewed against the new edition.

Questions

What documented information does ISO 9001 require?

The clause splits it in two: what the standard itself requires at specific points (the scope, the policy, objectives, and the records named in individual clauses such as audit results, management review results, calibration, nonconformity and corrective action, and design and production records), and whatever else the organization decides is necessary for the system to be effective. The standard does not require a quality manual or a fixed set of procedures.

Does ISO 9001 still require a quality manual?

No. The requirement for a quality manual was removed in the 2015 edition and the 2026 edition does not restore it. If you keep one, it is documented information you decided is necessary and it has to be controlled like anything else, including keeping its clause references current.

What is the difference between maintain and retain documented information?

In the 2015 edition, maintain meant documents you keep current, such as procedures, and retain meant records you keep as evidence. The 2026 edition replaced both phrases with wording about documented information being available, or available as evidence. The distinction between living documents and records still exists in practice; the words changed.

How long must ISO 9001 records be kept?

The standard does not set retention periods. Clause 7.5.3.2 requires you to address retention and disposition, which means deciding a period for each record type, recording that decision, and following it. Customer contracts, regulations and product liability considerations usually set the minimums.

Do electronic records need to be controlled differently?

The same requirements apply: availability, protection, access, change control, retention. For electronic records the questions become access permissions, backups, and whether changes are logged. A shared spreadsheet that anyone can edit without a trace fails the protection requirement whatever it contains.
